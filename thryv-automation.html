Thryv CRM · Automation Builder

Let the business run itself

Thryv's Automation Builder lets 200K small business owners set up workflows that run on their own. For example, a confirmation can go out the moment an appointment is booked. To stop splitting resources across two platforms, the business set out to consolidate on one unified platform. My job was to help around 38K accounts move to it, 95% of them certified partners. I helped design and ship core functions and improve the canvas features on the new platform to make the switch seamless and turn complex flows easy to build.

The problem

The product ran on two platforms, a legacy version built a decade ago and the newer Classic. Every improvement had to be designed, built and maintained twice, doubling the cost of every roadmap decision. Retiring Legacy would let the team put all of that investment into one platform. It only worked if partners moved with us.

Partners had the least reason to move. They are the certified agencies that run automations for their clients. Moving meant risking work their business depended on.

Partners were also the slowest builders.

Usage data showed partners took 45 minutes from edit to publish, 40% longer than small business owners who manage a single account, because their flows ran two times bigger. And we were asking them to migrate hundreds of those flows, on top of the work they already had.

Discovery

I ran a discovery workshop that pulled together partner interviews, in-app feedback, a journey map and competitor benchmarks. Building the workflow stood out as the biggest pain point.

The migration itself was never the blocker. The data carried over intact and stayed secure in transit. The real cost sat downstream of that. Once a flow outgrew the viewport, every incremental edit — finding a node, reading a label, rearranging a branch — compounded, and the effort to work around that friction only grew. And critically, that friction was identical on Classic and Legacy. Partners had no real incentive to switch. I picked up that signal and turned it into an opportunity.

I narrowed nine opportunities to three with stakeholders. Each one had to pass two tests. It had to make large partner flows easier to manage and faster to build, and it had to simplify building for small business owners.

Measuring success

To tie design to business value, I partnered with product and engineering on three goals to keep us on track.

  1. Drive adoption. Convert 40% of partner accounts from Legacy to Classic.
  2. Improve efficiency. Reduce partners' edit-to-publish time.
  3. Full UX audit and fix inconsistency. Turn one-off designs into shared components.

Each goal came back to the canvas, where partners spent most of their time.

Opportunity 1

Search on canvas

⚠️ In a flow of 20 sequences, partners spent minutes zooming and scanning just to find one sequence or spot an error.

I designed search over the canvas with status and sequence-type filters, so users can find any sequence by name or type and see its state at a glance, from errors to unpublished changes. I also pushed for a "last mile" detail that always brings the selected sequence into view, so the feature never looks broken.

Design process

Before ideating, I interviewed our pilot group to understand how they work today. From there I explored how search, filters and the canvas should work together. I reviewed each round of iterations on design details and search logic with engineering so the approach stayed feasible.

Non-negotiable Design Details

The difference between a feature that works and one that feels like it works.

On selection, the canvas measures the target’s position against the visible viewport and pans by the smallest offset that brings it fully into view, then applies the selected state. The rule holds for both node types, sequence and note.

  • Target occluded by the search panel: pan until it is centered in the region the panel leaves visible.
  • Target outside the viewport bounds: pan until it sits just inside the nearest edge.

Why I pushed hard for it

It follows a core usability heuristic, visibility of system status. When a user clicks a result and nothing visibly changes, the gulf of evaluation opens. They don’t infer the result is off-screen. They conclude the feature is broken.

Keeping movement minimal also preserves spatial context, so neighboring sequences stay in view and users keep their mental map of the flow. That polish drew the strongest praise in the demo.

Usability testing

In testing with 8 partners and 8 small business owners, users found sequences 60% faster. In the post-launch metric follow-up, edit-to-publish time dropped from 45 to 34 minutes, a 24% improvement.

Finding a sequence was no longer the problem. Reading the canvas still was.

Opportunity 2

Tidy-up

⚠️ Flows moved over from Legacy arrived with overlapping sequences and crossed lines, and every new branch meant rearranging by hand.

I designed Tidy-up, which arranges selected sequences into a clean, evenly spaced grid in one click.

I worked out the object placement logic and walked engineering through it, so they could start a feasibility spike while design was still in progress. The animation shows how Tidy-up places objects, following three rules.

  • Fill objects from left to right, then top to bottom.
  • Wrap to a new row when space runs out.
  • Size the grid from the number and size of the objects.

First, a blocker to be fixed

Sequence titles could wrap without limit, so a sequence had no maximum height and the grid had nothing reliable to measure. I capped titles at five lines, about 115 characters, which covers most sequence titles in existing workflows. Longer names end in an ellipsis on the canvas, scroll within the field while editing, and show in full in a tooltip on hover. That gave every sequence a fixed minimum and maximum size to place against. It also solved a pain point partners had already raised, long sequence names overlapping one another on the canvas, so this fix had to land first to set Tidy-up up for success.

Benchmarking to lock the scope

I studied how three other canvas-based tools handle arranging objects and turned the findings into a comparison chart. Walking stakeholders through it let us agree on what to include and what to leave out, which locked the scope before I started design exploration.

Technical constraint

When a backend constraint ruled out my first approach, I designed a toolbar on the selection that pairs Tidy-up with bulk duplicate and bulk delete, which also solved a separate complaint that users couldn't find those actions.

Two-quarter outcome

Alongside these features we shipped five improvements and met with partners regularly. Account managers checked in to help them through the move.

Together, that converted 58.5% of partner accounts from Legacy to Classic, beating our two-quarter goal of 40% and moving us toward retiring a decade of legacy infrastructure.

Converting partners was the near-term goal. The next one is staying ahead while every automation tool is moving towards AI.

Opportunity 3 · What's next

AI-assisted Decision Diamond

⚠️ The Decision Diamond decides which path a workflow takes, but in the current setup it is restricted to checking contact details. Small businesses run on jobs, estimates and appointments, so small business owners linked three flows with tags on contacts just to route an estimate by value. For partners, reading more than contact details would be a real upgrade to what they can automate. For small business owners the problem is discoverability. Fixing both would turn an underused feature into one that drives not just adoption but revenue. That is why we took it on next.

I redesigned it to read deals, quotes and appointments, with AI suggestions users review before applying. It’s a first step toward AI that builds whole workflows from a description.

During a PM transition I picked up the PM hat to spec four priority entities so the build kept moving. I tested the redesign with two groups, partners and small business owners, using a prototype wired to a live LLM so they judged real suggestions rather than scripted ones. That covered both halves of the problem: whether discoverability was solved, and whether each group got real value. After several rounds of debugging, QA and iteration, the feature is live with our alpha group. Beta and general availability are planned to follow.

AI Design Framework

I ran a workshop with product and engineering to collect their ideas for AI in the builder, and interviewed our pilot group for what they actually wanted. Together that came to 48 requests across five themes, from discovery and guidance to pre-launch checks. Every one sounded reasonable on its own, and they piled up faster than we could scope them.

I built a taxonomy. One axis is the level of autonomy, how much the AI does and how much work is left to the user, from executing to reviewing to approving. The other is reach, from a focused config change to a platform wide agent. Three things decide where a feature lands: how users work today, how mature our model is for that task, and how much reliance the output can reasonably carry. Routing logic in the Decision Diamond changes where work goes, so a person reviews each rule before it takes effect. Auto renaming a sequence is small enough to check at a glance, so the guardrail is lighter. The user still reads and confirms it, and it is fast and easy.

What I learned

While setting up usability testing with a scripted prototype, I quickly learned that one missed suggestion can erode trust and make the results lose credibility. So I worked with the backend team to connect the prototype to a live LLM, letting participants react to real suggestions.

I also wrote two guides to help other designers spin up prototypes with coded design system components in Claude. 55% of our design team has successfully onboarded.

AI beyond the prototype

The team has since brought AI into planning. We turn ideas into working solutions fast, put them in front of engineering early, and use that review to set the scope of each phase. Agreeing on scope is faster when everyone is reacting to something that works, not a description of it or a static wireframe.
